Gradient Descent

Elsewhere in this course we find a minimiser indirectly: assume one exists, derive the Euler–Lagrange equation, and solve that ODE. The direct method never forms the ODE. It treats \(I[y]\) as a function to be minimised, and minimises it the way you would minimise any function of many variables: start from a guess, work out which way each variable must move to make \(I\) smaller, move them all a little that way, and repeat.

Repeating that loop until nothing moves any more is gradient descent, and it is what the Descend button does. Nothing here solves for the answer; the curve is found by successive improvement, one small correction at a time.

Turning \(I[y]\) into an ordinary function

Replace the curve by an \(N\)-node polyline on \(x_k=a+kh\). Pin \(y_0\) and \(y_N\) at the boundary values; the interior heights \(y_1,\dots,y_{N-1}\) are just \(N-1\) real numbers.

A polyline is a curve like any other, so \(I\) has a value on it. To estimate this we split the integral into pieces, one per segment \([x_k,x_{k+1}]\). Over segment \([x_k,x_{k+1}]\) the polyline is a straight line, so its slope there is exactly \(p_k=(y_{k+1}-y_k)/h\) at every point of the segment, and its height runs from \(y_k\) to \(y_{k+1}\). The integral is the only thing left, and in general it cannot be done exactly, so we estimate it the simplest way there is: the width \(h\) times the integrand measured halfway along.

\[ \int_{x_k}^{x_{k+1}}\! F(x,y,y')\,dx \;\approx\; F\big(\bar x_k,\,\bar y_k,\, p_k\big)\,h \]

That is the midpoint rule, with \(\bar x_k = x_k+\tfrac h2\) the middle of the segment and \(\bar y_k = \tfrac12(y_k+y_{k+1})\) the polyline's height there — which is the average of the two endpoint heights because the segment is straight.

Adding up all \(N\) segments gives a number attached to the \(N-1\) heights:

\[ I_N(y_1,\dots,y_{N-1}) \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} F\big(\bar x_k,\; \bar y_k,\; p_k\big)\, h. \]

That is an ordinary function \(\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\to\mathbb{R}\): give it \(N-1\) numbers and it returns one. Minimising it needs no calculus of variations at all — only its partial derivatives.

The step the page takes

One press of Step moves every interior node once:

\[ y_j \;\longleftarrow\; y_j \,-\, t\,\frac{\partial I_N}{\partial y_j}, \qquad j=1,\dots,N-1. \]

Each node moves against its own partial derivative, which is the direction that makes \(I_N\) smaller. The step size \(t\) is not fixed: it is halved until \(I_N\) really does decrease, so the curve can never overshoot and jump uphill. Descend just repeats this step over and over until the derivatives are zero; the Steps per redraw slider sets how many it gets through between refreshes of the plots, so turning it up makes the curve settle sooner in real time without changing anything about the method.

Where Euler–Lagrange comes from

Here is the point of the page. The height \(y_j\) appears in exactly two terms of that sum: segment \(j-1\), where it is the right-hand node, and segment \(j\), where it is the left-hand one. Differentiating just those two,

\[ \frac{\partial I_N}{\partial y_j} = \frac{h}{2}\Big(F_y^{(j-1)}\!+F_y^{(j)}\Big) - \Big(F_{y'}^{(j)}\!-F_{y'}^{(j-1)}\Big), \]

and dividing by \(h\) turns the second bracket into a difference quotient:

\[ \frac{1}{h}\frac{\partial I_N}{\partial y_j} = F_y - \frac{d}{dx}\big(F_{y'}\big) + O(h). \]

The discrete gradient is the Euler–Lagrange expression. Setting \(\nabla I_N = 0\) — the ordinary first-derivative test — becomes the Euler–Lagrange equation as \(N\to\infty\). It was never pulled out of a hat.

That is what the lower plot shows: the residual at node \(j\) is exactly \(h^{-1}\partial I_N/\partial y_j\) — the same derivative the step above uses. When every one of them is zero no node has any reason to move, the polyline stops changing, and the Euler–Lagrange equation is satisfied. Watch the plot flatten onto zero.

Why \(I_N\) does not equal \(I\)

Even once the descent has converged, \(I_N\) differs from \(I\) in the fourth decimal or so, and can land on either side of it. That is not a failed minimisation: a polyline with \(N\) nodes cannot be the exact curve, so \(I_N\to I\) only as \(N\to\infty\). Drag \(N\) upwards and watch the difference shrink.

Relation to the stationarity page

The Euler–Lagrange explorer plots \(I(\varepsilon)\) along one chosen variation \(\eta\) — that is the directional derivative \(\nabla I_N\cdot\eta\). Here every node moves at once, along \(-\nabla I_N\), which is the single direction in which \(I_N\) falls fastest. Same object: that page tests one direction you choose, this one finds the best direction there is.

One segment, one rectangle

On the left is one segment of the polyline; on the right is the integrand \(F(x,y,y')\) along it, which varies because \(x\) and \(y\) do — though \(y'\) does not, being the fixed slope \(p_k\). The midpoint rule replaces the area under that curve by the shaded rectangle: one measurement of \(F\), taken in the middle, times the width \(h\). Summing one such rectangle per segment is exactly the \(I_N\) being descended.

This panel runs at its own node count, set below and starting at four, on the curve you are descending resampled that coarsely — at the working \(N\) a segment is far too short to see anything. Watch whether the blue curve comes out straight or bent. Where \(F\) is straight along the segment, measuring in the middle catches its average exactly and the rectangle is right however coarse the polyline; where \(F\) bends, the rectangle misses, and the miss shrinks as you add nodes.
