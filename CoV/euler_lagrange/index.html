Is it stationary?

The Euler–Lagrange equation comes from a single idea: if \(f\) really is an extremal of \(I[y]\), then nudging it in any admissible direction cannot decrease \(I\) to first order.

Fix a variation \(\eta\) with \(\eta(a)=\eta(b)=0\) and set \(y = f + \varepsilon\eta\). Then \(I\) becomes an ordinary function of one variable \(\varepsilon\), and the condition is:

\[ \left.\frac{dI}{d\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0} = 0. \]

Pick a problem, a candidate and a variation in the panel on the right, then drag \(\varepsilon\). The lower plot is \(I(\varepsilon)\). For an extremal, it should be flat at the origin.

Why every \(\eta\) matters

Stationarity in one direction is not enough. The Euler–Lagrange equation is equivalent to \(\delta I = 0\) for every admissible \(\eta\) — that is exactly what the Fundamental Lemma needs in order to conclude anything about \(f\) itself.

The first variation

The slope \(\delta I\) in the panel is the quantity that appears in the middle of the proof, just before the integration by parts:

\[ \delta I = \int_a^b \left( \frac{\partial F}{\partial y}\,\eta + \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'}\,\eta' \right) dx \]

Requiring it to vanish for every admissible \(\eta\) is what yields the Euler–Lagrange equation.
