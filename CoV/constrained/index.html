Constrained Minimisation

The direct method minimises \(I[y]\) over every curve joining the two endpoints. Most of the classical problems are not like that: they come with a side condition the curve must also satisfy — a chain has a fixed length, a fence encloses a fixed area. The quantity to be minimised and the quantity that is fixed are both integrals of the curve.

Why you cannot just descend

Throughout this page \(I\) is the functional being minimised and \(J\) the one held fixed. Take the hanging chain. It settles so as to minimise its potential energy, which for a uniform chain of unit density is

\[ I[y] \;=\; \int_a^b y\,\sqrt{1+(y')^2}\;dx. \]

Descend that on its own and the chain simply falls: dropping any part of it lowers \(I\), and nothing stops the process, so \(I\) is unbounded below and there is no minimiser at all. The problem only becomes well posed once the length is fixed:

\[ J[y] = \int_a^b \sqrt{1+(y')^2}\,dx = c. \]

\(c\) is the value the constraint is held at, and it is what the second slider sets. It is a length for the chain and for the fence, and an area for the dual problem, so rather than switch letters from problem to problem the page calls it \(c\) throughout.

Run the page with the chain and watch the top two numbers in the panel: they are \(I_N\) and \(J_N\), the two integrals evaluated on the polyline. \(J_N\) is pulled to \(c\) and stays there; \(I_N\) drops steeply and then drifts down. It does not fall at every single step — the descent minimises \(K\) below, not \(I\) on its own, so the energy is free to edge back up whenever the constraint hauls the chain in. It is the constraint, not the energy, that decides how far the chain can drop.

Both integrals become ordinary functions

Exactly as on the direct method page, replace the curve by an \(N\)-node polyline and estimate each integral by the midpoint rule on each segment. Note that both integrals get the same treatment, and both come out as ordinary functions of the same \(N-1\) heights:

\[ I_N(y_1,\dots,y_{N-1}), \qquad J_N(y_1,\dots,y_{N-1}). \]

The problem is now entirely finite: minimise \(I_N\) subject to \(J_N=c\), over \(N-1\) real numbers. No functionals, no curves — a function of many variables with one equation of constraint.

Which is a problem you have already solved

That is exactly the situation on the Lagrange multipliers page: minimise \(f\) subject to \(g=\)const. The answer there is that at a constrained minimum the two gradients must be parallel, \(\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g\) — if they were not, you could slide along the constraint surface and do better. Nothing in that argument cares how many variables there are, so here it reads

\[ \nabla I_N \;=\; \lambda\, \nabla J_N , \]

or, one component at a time, at each interior node \(j\),

\[ \frac{\partial I_N}{\partial y_j} \;=\; \lambda\,\frac{\partial J_N}{\partial y_j}. \]

Where constrained Euler–Lagrange comes from

Here is the point of the page. Divide both sides by \(h\). The direct method page worked out what that does to a sum of this kind: only the two segments containing \(y_j\) involve it, and differentiating them gives

\[ \frac1h\frac{\partial I_N}{\partial y_j} = F_y - \frac{d}{dx}\big(F_{y'}\big) + O(h). \]

\(J_N\) is a sum of exactly the same shape, built from its own integrand \(G\), so the identical argument applies to it word for word. The parallel-gradient condition therefore becomes, as \(N\to\infty\),

\[ F_y - \frac{d}{dx}F_{y'} \;=\; \lambda\Big(G_y - \frac{d}{dx}G_{y'}\Big), \]

and that is the Euler–Lagrange equation of \(K = I - \lambda J\), whose integrand is \(F - \lambda G\).

So the multiplier rule for functionals was never a separate principle. It is the ordinary \(\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g\) you already know, applied in \(\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\), in the limit. Constrained Euler–Lagrange stands to Lagrange multipliers exactly as Euler–Lagrange stands to the first-derivative test.

That is what the lower-left plot shows. The residual at node \(j\) is \(h^{-1}\big(\partial I_N/\partial y_j - \lambda\,\partial J_N/\partial y_j\big)\) — the gap between the two gradients, one node at a time, and the panel reports its largest value. Watch it flatten onto zero: when it does, the gradients really are parallel. When it reaches zero and \(J_N=c\), both halves of the problem are solved at once.

Finding \(\lambda\)

One thing is still missing. Parallel gradients pin down the shape of the answer, but not the number \(\lambda\) itself: that is fixed only by the requirement that \(J_N=c\) actually holds. So \(\lambda\) has to be found alongside the curve, and the rest of this panel is how.

If \(\lambda\) were known there would be nothing left to do — descend \(K_N=I_N-\lambda J_N\) and stop. It is not known, and that is the whole difficulty. While the running guess at \(\lambda\) is wrong, descending \(K_N\) has no reason to respect the constraint at all: guess too small a \(\lambda\) for the chain and length is too cheap, so the curve just falls. Something has to hold it near \(J_N=c\) while \(\lambda\) is still being found.

The penalty

So a penalty is attached, and the descent runs on

\[ K_\mu \;=\; K_N \;+\; \tfrac{\mu}{2}\,(J_N-c)^2 . \]

The added term is zero when the constraint holds and grows as the square of the violation, so its gradient is a restoring pull back towards \(J_N=c\) whose strength is proportional to how far off the curve has got. It is a spring rather than a wall: the curve is allowed to break the constraint, but it pays for doing so, and the further out it goes the harder it is pulled back.

Every 20 steps the multiplier is then nudged by

\[ \lambda \;\longleftarrow\; \lambda - \mu\,(J_N - c). \]

Read that in words: if the curve is coming out too long, raise the price of length; if it is too short, lower it.

Note what is not changed by any of this. The penalty vanishes at the answer, where \(J_N=c\) exactly, so the curve the descent settles on is a stationary point of \(K_N\) itself — which is to say it satisfies \(\nabla I_N = \lambda\nabla J_N\), exactly what the argument above asked for. The penalty is scaffolding for getting there, not part of the answer.

What \(\mu\) is, and why 20 steps

\(\mu>0\) is the stiffness of that spring. Notice that the same \(\mu\) is also the step size in the \(\lambda\) update, which is not a coincidence: that update is a gradient step in \(\lambda\), and taking it at the penalty's own strength is what makes the curve and the multiplier converge together. One consequence is that \(\mu=0\) does not merely switch the penalty off — it freezes \(\lambda\) at \(0\), and nothing is ever found.

It is a dial with a wide flat bottom rather than a knife edge. At \(N=40\) the chain converges in 2946 steps at \(\mu=2\), 3547 at the \(50\) this page uses, 6403 at \(200\) and 26900 at \(1000\).

How is it chosen? By hand, once per problem, and then held fixed for the whole descent: \(50\) for the two length constraints and \(200\) for the area one, each picked from a sweep like that one and sitting comfortably inside its basin rather than at the very bottom. There is no formula, and \(\mu\) is not dimensionless — it multiplies a squared constraint violation, so a problem that fixes a length and a problem that fixes an area genuinely need different numbers.

Should it not adapt? The textbook augmented-Lagrangian method does raise \(\mu\) as it runs, stiffening the penalty whenever the constraint is still violated after the inner minimisation has been solved. That last clause is the catch: this page never solves an inner problem. It descends continuously and nudges \(\lambda\) every 20 steps, so “the violation did not fall much over the last 20 steps” usually means the descent has not got there yet, not that the penalty is too soft. Stiffening on that signal pushes \(\mu\) up into the ill-conditioned range and makes matters worse: implemented and measured, it drove \(\mu\) past \(10^4\) and failed to converge on 6 of 27 runs across the three problems, against 27 of 27 for the constant. Adapting \(\mu\) honestly would need an inner/outer loop, and the animation would stop being one continuous descent.

The 20 is the one constant here that has a sharp edge on one side. The update assumes the curve has had a chance to settle at the current price; update after every single step and price and curve chase each other. Measured on the chain, \(\lambda\) then swings between \(-21.6\) and \(+6.0\) instead of settling at \(-1.2653\), and the descent never converges at all. Past that the cadence is not delicate — every 2 steps, every 20 and every 200 all converge in much the same number of steps. Twenty is a comfortable margin, not a tuned value.

\(\lambda\) starts at \(0\), which is to say the page starts by pretending there is no constraint at all. Watch the lower plot: the value it settles on is a genuine discovery of the algorithm, and it agrees with the analytic multiplier printed beneath it.

Why \(I_N\) does not equal \(I\)

As on the direct method page, a polyline with \(N\) nodes is not the exact curve, so the converged values differ from the analytic ones in the fourth decimal or so and shrink like \(h^2\) as \(N\) grows. The same goes for \(\lambda\).

Where this sits

Three pages, one idea seen three ways. The Euler–Lagrange page asks whether a given curve is stationary; the direct method finds the stationary curve by descending; this page does the same when the curve is not free to be anything it likes. The Lagrange multipliers page is not an analogy but the thing itself — its \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\) is what this page discretises — and Dido's problem and Soap Bubbles show the closed-form answers this method reaches numerically.
