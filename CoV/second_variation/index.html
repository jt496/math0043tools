Is It a Minimum?

Solving the Euler–Lagrange equation gives a stationary curve, and that is all it gives.

What stationary means

It has a precise meaning. Take any variation \(\eta\) that vanishes at both endpoints, so that \(y+\varepsilon\eta\) is another curve joining the same two points. Then

\[ \left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon} I[y+\varepsilon\eta]\right|_{\varepsilon=0} = 0 . \]

One number, zero, for each choice of \(\eta\) — and it holds for every admissible \(\eta\), not merely some. In words: the rate at which \(I\) changes as you set off from \(y\) is zero, whichever direction you set off in. Nothing beyond that is being claimed.

The condition that \(\eta\) vanish at the endpoints is not a technicality: it says what the curve is being compared with. Integrating by parts leaves \(\delta I = [F_{y'}\eta]_a^b + \int(F_y - \tfrac{d}{dx}F_{y'})\eta\,dx\), and it is the pinned endpoints that kill the first piece. Let \(\eta\) move the ends and the same catenary is no longer stationary.

Why that is not enough

In one variable the same statement is \(f'(x)=0\), and you already know what it does not settle: a stationary point may be a minimum, a maximum, or neither, and the first derivative alone cannot tell you which.

Curves are no different. The first variation is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one, and deciding between the cases needs the second derivative. That is all this page does.

The test you already know

Discretise as on the direct method page: an \(N\)-node polyline turns \(I[y]\) into an ordinary function \(I_N\) of the \(N-1\) interior heights. A stationary curve is a point where \(\nabla I_N = 0\), and for a function of several variables the second-derivative test is the one from multivariable calculus — look at the matrix of second partials, the Hessian:

\[ H_{ij} \;=\; \frac{\partial^2 I_N}{\partial y_i\,\partial y_j}. \]

\(H\) is symmetric, so it has real eigenvalues, and the rule is the familiar one:

  • every eigenvalue \(>0\) — a minimum;
  • some eigenvalue \(<0\) — not a minimum;
  • an eigenvalue at \(0\) — the test is inconclusive, as ever.

The Hessian is tridiagonal

\(y_j\) appears in only two terms of the sum, the segments on either side of it, so \(\partial^2 I_N/\partial y_i\partial y_j\) is zero unless \(i\) and \(j\) are neighbours. \(H\) is therefore tridiagonal, and its whole spectrum is cheap to compute however large \(N\) is. That is the lower-left plot.

What a negative eigenvalue is

Here is the point of the page. An eigenvalue \(\kappa<0\) comes with an eigenvector \(v\), and Taylor's theorem along that direction gives

\[ \begin{aligned} I_N(y+\varepsilon v) = I_N(y) &+ \tfrac12\,\varepsilon^2\, v^{\mathsf T} H v \\[2pt] &+ O(\varepsilon^3), \end{aligned} \]

with no first-order term because the curve is stationary. For an eigenvector \(v^{\mathsf T}Hv = \kappa\|v\|^2\), which carries the sign of \(\kappa\). So moving along \(v\) makes \(I_N\) smaller, and the curve cannot be a minimum. That eigenvector is not an abstraction: it is drawn on the upper plot, and the \(\varepsilon\) slider moves the curve along it. The lower-right panel plots \(I_N\) as you do so — a parabola opening upwards at a minimum, downwards when the test fails.

How exact is that expansion?

The \(O(\varepsilon^3)\) is what pulls the lower-right curve away from a true parabola once \(\varepsilon\) is large. For the oscillator it is identically zero — there \(I_N\) really is quadratic, so the parabola is exact — and for the straight line it happens to vanish as well, leaving \(O(\varepsilon^4)\).

The first-order term is also not quite zero, and that is what the residual in the panel measures. This page plots the curve that solves Euler–Lagrange exactly, and such a curve is only \(O(h)\)-stationary for the discretised \(I_N\), so \(\varepsilon\,\nabla I_N\!\cdot v\) is small rather than absent: about \(8\times10^{-5}\) for the fat-necked film at \(N=60\), against a quadratic coefficient of \(0.58\). Raise \(N\) and it falls; for the oscillator, whose \(y\equiv0\) is stationary for \(I_N\) as well, it is zero outright.

The eigenvector is exactly the perturbation \(\eta\) that the Euler–Lagrange page invites you to try by hand. There, you pick \(\eta\) and see what it does to \(I\). Here, the Hessian hands you the worst \(\eta\) there is, without your having to guess.

What this is called

Classically all of this travels under the second variation, and the moment an eigenvalue crosses zero is a conjugate point, governed by the Jacobi condition. None of that machinery is needed above: once the functional is an ordinary function of finitely many variables, the ordinary second-derivative test answers the question, and the classical statements are what it becomes as \(N\to\infty\).

Where this sits

The first variation page tests one direction at a time and asks whether \(I\) is stationary; the direct method finds a stationary curve by descending; this page asks what kind of stationary point it is. Together they are the first- and second-derivative tests, in \(N-1\) variables.
